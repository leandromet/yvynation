# Synthesis — what the four territories share and why they differ

This note distils the cross-case argument of chapter 11, tying it back to the
per-territory numbers in [RESULTS.md](RESULTS.md) and the method in
[METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md).

---

## 1. The longitudinal argument

Read coast-to-west, the four territories trace one line: **the later the
critical juncture arrived relative to the frontier, the more forest the policy
was able to keep.** The geographic order is also a policy-history order and a
"March to the West" frontier order.

| Territory | Biome | Critical juncture | Title | Extent | Forest | Deforest. | One-line story |
|---|---|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Krenak | Atlantic Forest | 1808 | 1997 | 20,034 ha | ~11% | ~89% | Recovery on exhausted land |
| Bacurizinho | Cerrado–Amazon | 1901 | 1983 / 2008 | 135,346 ha | ~78% | ~12% | Boundary-effect experiment |
| Kayapó | Amazon | 1970s | 1991 | 3,283,191 ha | ~93% | ~5% | Extractive lockstep, intact core |
| Betânia | Amazon | 1960s | 1995 | 126,066 ha | ~93% | ~2% | Hydrological re-classification |

A territory that lost protection early and spent a century under extractive
tenure shows ~89% deforestation; one that entered the post-Constitution regime
nearly intact shows 2–5%. Biome contributes (the Atlantic Forest was already
fragmented before the satellite era) but **biome alone cannot explain an 87 pp
gap** — the buffer contrast (§4) shows unprotected surroundings evolved very
differently under the same biome.

## 2. What each territory teaches

- **Krenak — recognition without resource.** Policy recognition is necessary but
  insufficient once path dependence has exhausted what the policy protects. The
  1997 STF ruling returned the title, not the forest; the post-1988 story is
  slow recovery of degraded land, plus a non-policy shock (Fundão 2015) the
  pipeline must accommodate.
- **Bacurizinho — tenure completeness.** The *same* legal category yields
  different pixel outcomes when tenure clarity differs. The 1.75–1.91×
  4901-vs-4902 differential is the cleanest internal proof that physical
  demarcation and resolution of overlapping CAR claims — not the legal label —
  is what bites.
- **Kayapó — institutional capacity.** Three million hectares held above 91%
  cover against an aggressive frontier is not natural; it is warrior strategy,
  the Altamira Gathering, Instituto Raoni/Kabu, guard posts. But the 2019→2020
  loss uptick shows even that is not autonomous from national politics, and the
  mining-pasture lockstep is the live pressure front.
- **Betânia — the upper bound.** Where stewardship and recent recognition run
  cleanly, the record shows what an effective regime looks like: >92% cover, a
  confirmed *null* result for COVID-2020, and a dominant signal that is
  hydrological, not agricultural.

## 3. Policy lag has three temporal signatures

The framework's "lag" claim survives the data — but the lag is **not a single
number**. The Yvynation outputs let three distinct kinds be told apart:

| Signature | Definition | Cleanest examples |
|---|---|---|
| **(a) Fast lag (1–2 yr)** | A discrete national event → next-year Hansen canopy hit | Krenak 2012 Code → 2014 loss; Kayapó 2016 PEC215/Belo Monte → 2017 (73,644 ha); Kayapó 2019 rollback → 2020 (32,074 ha) |
| **(b) Persistent / boundary effect** | Visible in the 2001–2023 loss **percentage**, not any single year | Bacurizinho 4901 vs 4902 (1.91×); Kayapó mining-pasture lockstep (+1,352% each) |
| **(c) Gradual re-classification** | Multi-year MapBiomas class drift no single year can carry | Krenak forest recovery (+141%); Betânia Floodable Forest → Wetland (+223%) |

If the lag were zero, policy would have to anticipate satellite coverage; if it
were infinite/random, path dependence would carry no explanatory content. The
finite, patterned lag is exactly the "policy-to-pixel signature" the framework
was built to capture — and separating the three signatures is one of the
project's main methodological results.

## 4. The buffer protective effect is real and quantifiable

Using the 10 km exclusive buffer as a counterfactual (see METHODOLOGY §5), the
May 2026 Collection 10 batch (`multi_window.md`) shows the interior–buffer
divergence directly, and it is consistent:

| Region pair | Territory forest Δ | Buffer forest Δ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bacurizinho 4901 | −1.3 pp | **−25.5 pp** |
| Bacurizinho 4902 | −6.2 pp | −22.0 pp |
| Kayapó NE (hot spot) | −0.9 pp | **−65.2 pp** (91% → 26% forest) |
| Kayapó NW | −0.2 pp | −21.7 pp |
| Kayapó SE | −1.2 pp | −31.3 pp |
| Betânia | −1.6 pp | −1.4 pp (near-identical) |
| Krenak Sete Salões | **+6.4 pp** (regrowth) | +0.2 pp |

The pattern maps onto the gradient: the contrast is sharpest where the frontier
is hottest (Kayapó NE/SE, Bacurizinho), *absent* where the surroundings are
still deep forest (Betânia), and *inverted* where the territory is recovering
inside an already-cleared matrix (Krenak). The sharp satellite-visible line at
the boundary means enforcement works at the limit — and that the actors clearing
land plan around that line using the same spatial data these analyses use.

## 5. Same national laws, different outcomes — four structural conditions

The 1965 Code, 1988 Constitution, 2004 PPCDAm and 2012 Code appear in all four
policy-lag tables, yet bite differently. Four conditions determine whether a
national rule has a local effect:

1. **Path-dependence depth** — how exhausted was the resource when the rule
   arrived? (Krenak: nothing left to protect; Betânia: everything intact.)
2. **Tenure completeness** — was declaration backed by physical demarcation and
   resolution of private claims? (Bacurizinho's expansion is the counter-example.)
3. **Institutional capacity on the ground** — organisation, resources,
   visibility to enforce the boundary. (Kayapó.)
4. **Biome & market proximity** — recovery ceilings and propagule pressure
   differ between a fragmented Atlantic Forest matrix and intact Amazon.

These are not simply additive: the **absence of any one can defeat the others**.
Bacurizinho has capacity and an intact biome, but incomplete tenure let its
expansion deforest at buffer-like rates. Path dependence is the strongest single
predictor — *but not destiny*: Kayapó and Betânia show that early recognition
plus institutional response can reset a trajectory.

## 6. Comparative valuation — the economic stakes of the gradient

| Territory | Land market value (USD) | Annual ES value (USD) | ES as % of land value |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Krenak | $41.9 M | $8.2 M | 19.6% |
| Bacurizinho | $211.5 M | $55.5 M | 26.2% |
| Kayapó | $7,685.8 M | $1,346.1 M | 17.5% |
| Betânia | $19.5 M | $51.7 M | **265.0%** |

Two structural facts fall out of the gradient:

- **The Betânia inversion.** Remote deep-forest land has the lowest per-hectare
  market price ("General" regional typology) but the same ~410 USD/ha/yr
  regulating-service value as anywhere — so its ecosystem-service value is 2.6×
  its market value. The opposite logic hits Krenak: urban proximity inflates
  land price while 89% deforestation suppresses ES potential, making it the
  territory where the opportunity cost of conversion was most fully realised and
  the recovery cost is now highest.
- **Kayapó's magnitude.** ~$1.3 B/yr in ecosystem services (≈$410/ha × 3.28 M ha)
  dwarfs the other three combined — and still sits below the ~797 USD/ha/yr
  opportunity cost of agricultural conversion, which is precisely why the
  surrounding frontier keeps pushing and the institutional network must keep
  absorbing the pressure.

---

## 7. Bottom line for the thesis

Path dependence explains both the **persistence of degradation** (Krenak) and
the **possibility of trajectory change** (Kayapó, Betânia). The critical-juncture
concept does not predict equilibrium; it predicts that the cost of changing a
trajectory rises with its depth — so **early intervention is more efficient than
late recognition**. The Yvynation batch is the instrument that makes this
measurable and reproducible for any territory in the FUNAI (or CNUC) database.
