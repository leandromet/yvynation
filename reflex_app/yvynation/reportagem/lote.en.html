A hundred territories in an afternoon: batch processing, step by step

Sometimes the question is not about one land but about many: every Indigenous land in a state, every extractive reserve in a biome. For that, Yvynation runs the full analysis on up to a hundred territories at once and hands everything back in a single ZIP. This walkthrough goes through each screen of a real run and ends with what even a small batch can answer.

1

Pick many areas at once

The batch starts from the list: Indigenous lands or conservation units, with each one's people, phase, state and area. The search and the filters (state, phase, modality, category, level, group, area range) narrow the list; Select all filtered takes the whole cut. You can also paste or upload a list of names.

This example run uses two small lands in Mato Grosso do Sul: Jaguari (405 ha) and Guaimbé (717 ha). Small areas finish in minutes; a batch of a hundred large lands takes hours.

In the app: home page → the Batch Processing card, Select step. Direct link: /?modo=lote.

2

Check before spending an afternoon

A large batch takes time and Earth Engine quota. The Review button shows the whole selection, with each territory's area and state, before anything is computed. When you start, the app asks for confirmation once more.

In the app: the Review button, next to the n/100 selected count.

3

Years, analyses, figures and the surrounding zone

The settings apply to every territory in the batch: the MapBiomas years (one, or two to compare), the Hansen GLAD year, which analyses to run (MapBiomas, comparison between years, transition treemaps, Hansen GLAD and GFC, PNG maps), the figures, the multi-year window, the timeline and the buffer zone.

Every territory gets exactly the same sums, which makes the batch comparable row by row.

In the app: the Configure step, groups Years, Analyses, Figures and images, Multi-window, Timeline and Buffer zone.

4

Running unattended

Once started, the app processes the territories in parallel, logs each stage and retries automatically when Earth Engine turns a request down. You can leave the tab open and go do something else: the batch is not paused while it runs.

In the app: the Start Batch Processing button in the bottom bar; the Run step, with progress per territory.

5

One file, with everything inside

At the end, one ZIP holds a folder per territory: the readme, the run's metadata (years, analyses, source versions), the tables and the figures. The same package is kept under Previous Runs, to download again later.

In the app: the download button at the end of the Run step, and Previous Runs at the top.

6

With the same sums in every row, the question becomes comparative. Here, the eight registered Indigenous lands of Mato Grosso do Sul between 200 and 1,000 ha, each with its native vegetation inside and in the 10 km around it, in the latest MapBiomas year.

What the batch does not say

  • It does not rank territories by merit. The table compares cover; each land's or unit's situation depends on history, people, category and pressure, which do not fit in a column.
  • It does not merge overlapping areas. Each territory is analysed with its own outline; lands and units that overlap count the same area twice, and the batch total is not the area of the set.
  • It does not replace reading each one. The batch is a first pass; a land that stands out deserves the individual analysis, with the map and the timeline.
  • It does not run Canada. Batch processing covers Brazil; Canada's protected areas have their own page.

Notes

  1. The territories processed so far range from 4,282 ha (Krenak) to 3,283,191 ha (Kayapó), a factor of about 770. So the batch does not split the list into fixed chunks: each worker takes the next territory from a shared queue (asyncio.Queue), and no one sits idle on small lands while another is still on the largest. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  2. The batch works on three levels: territories in parallel, taken from the queue; each territory's analyses sent to Earth Engine together, up to 11 requests at once; and maps and charts through one drawing lane per plotting library. Measured on a 31-territory run with 2 vCPU and 8 GiB, Earth Engine was busy 9.8% of the time and the drawing lane 80.1%: a 644 MB archive in 10.6 minutes. More cores would barely help; the next gain is in drawing faster. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  3. Areas above 1,000,000 ha are split into four quadrants, because Earth Engine timed out on the ring for areas above about 2 million ha. Before building the PDF, the batch adds the four pieces' classes, transitions and yearly loss back together and recomputes the percentages, so the report describes the land the catalogue names. If a report fails, the batch logs the failure and keeps that area's analysis. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  4. The surrounding ring is exclusive (it leaves the land itself out) and is there to compare, not to prove an effect. Clearing pushed outside the boundary inflates the contrast, and protected areas tend to be placed on land that was less threatened to begin with (Ewers & Rodrigues, 2008; Joppa & Pfaff, 2009). Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  5. Statistics about named Indigenous lands, published under an open licence, can be quoted in contexts the communities did not choose. The CARE principles for Indigenous data governance (Carroll et al., 2020) ask more than that, and the suite does not yet consult Indigenous organisations on how results are presented. The paper records this as a gap, not a completed obligation. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
