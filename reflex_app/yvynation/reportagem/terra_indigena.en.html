How to read an Indigenous land in Yvynation, from the inside out

Pick a land from FUNAI's catalogue and Yvynation returns forty years of MapBiomas, the forest loss measured by Hansen, a timeline of clearing, regrowth and fire, and the same reading for the 10 km around it. This walkthrough opens a real Indigenous land and follows, in the app's own order, where each number comes from and what it does not say.

1

Pick the land from FUNAI's catalogue

Yvynation holds the 657 Indigenous lands in FUNAI's catalogue, with people, phase and demarcation dates. Search by name, filter by state or click a land on the map. The outline comes from the catalogue itself, without going through Earth Engine.

In the app: on the home page, the Indigenous Lands Analysis card; in the sidebar, Study area, with the search and the list. Direct link: /?modo=terra-indigena&busca=….

2

Ten kilometres around, for comparison

A number inside the area only means something next to what happens outside it. As soon as the area is chosen, Yvynation builds a 10 km ring outward from the boundary on its own and runs the same analyses in it.

In the app: the Buffer Zone panel in the sidebar: the 10 km ring comes with the area, and another radius can be requested under Create.

3

Inside and out, in the latest year

MapBiomas Collection 11 classifies every 30 m pixel in Brazil every year. Each class's area comes from each pixel's true size, not a fixed 900 m². The bars compare each class's share inside and around.

In the app: the Run all analyses button at the top, and the MapBiomas tab under Results.

4

Forty years in three snapshots

The same sum in 1985, 2005 and 2025: how much of each zone is native vegetation (forest, savanna and grassland). The map shows the series' first year, to compare with the previous step.

In the app: the Compare tab (two years side by side) and the Multi-window tab (several years at once).

5

What became what

The diagram links each 1985 class to the class the same pixel had in 2025. A band linking a class to itself is what stayed the same; the bands that cross are the change.

In the app: the Compare tab, with the transition matrix, and the Treemaps tab, one per class.

6

A second opinion on the forest

Hansen/UMD measures something else: tree cover, not land-use classes. It starts from the 2000 canopy, dates the year each pixel lost its trees and adds an undated gain. When MapBiomas and Hansen tell the same story, the story is firmer.

In the app: the Hansen GFC tab, and the Hansen/GLAD tab for a single year's cover.

7

Year by year: clearing, regrowth and fire

The timeline brings together four yearly series: primary vegetation clearing and regrowth (MapBiomas Deforestation and Secondary Vegetation), canopy loss (Hansen) and fire scars (MapBiomas Fire).

The green line marks ratification. The series does not say what ratification caused, but it puts before and after on the same scale.

In the app: the Timeline tab, which can overlay the political context (federal and state governments) and ENSO.

8

The same line, inside and out

To compare zones of different sizes, clearing becomes hectares per 1,000 hectares of each zone. If the outside line rises and the inside one does not, the boundary is holding; if both rise together, the pressure has already crossed it.

In the app: the Timeline tab, with the buffer zone switched on.

9

Report, tables and maps to take away

Everything in the tabs can leave the app: a PDF report, CSV tables, PNG maps and charts, all in one ZIP. The data Yvynation produces are open (CC BY 4.0), with the source of each layer.

In the app: the Export & Download panel below the results, and How to Cite at the top.

What Yvynation does not say

  • It does not say who cleared or why. The series shows where and when cover changed. Invasion, mining, leasing or management fire can only be told apart with field information.
  • It does not speak for the people. Land cover is not lived territory. Swiddens, paths and traditional management fire show up as use, and the people who live there are the ones who know how to read these numbers.
  • The 10 km zone is not a legal buffer zone. It is a comparison ring, the same for every land, to see whether pressure from outside reaches the boundary.
  • The boundary is the catalogue's. The outline is the one FUNAI publishes. Lands under study or with contested limits may be drawn differently on the ground.
  • It cannot see below 30 m. Small clearings and degradation under the canopy escape both MapBiomas and Hansen.

Notes

  1. FUNAI, Brazil's federal agency for Indigenous peoples, identifies and demarcates the lands the 1988 Constitution recognises as traditionally occupied by Indigenous peoples. Each boundary moves through legal phases, from study to delimitation, declaration and ratification by presidential decree, and FUNAI's public layer records the phase. An official, stable and enumerable boundary is what makes it possible to compare lands across the country and to run them all at once, in batch. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  2. The ring is exclusive: it starts at the boundary and leaves the area itself out, so no hectare is counted in both zones. Even so, it is a comparison region, not a counterfactual. Clearing pushed from inside to outside (leakage) inflates the contrast, and protected areas tend to be placed on land that was less threatened to begin with (Ewers & Rodrigues, 2008; Joppa & Pfaff, 2009). So the screen compares, but does not credit the difference to the boundary. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  3. Area comes from pixelArea(), each pixel's true area, not from the pixel count times 0.09 ha. MapBiomas pixels are measured in degrees, and their area shrinks away from the Equator. The convention started in Naturametrics and reached Yvynation in August 2026, one of the modules that move between the suite's three tools. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  4. MapBiomas and Hansen have to stay separate because they measure different things. An April 2026 export put 305,250 ha of Hansen loss in 2024 inside the Kayapó Indigenous Land, about two-thirds of all the 2001–2023 loss in a single year. It was a wildfire scar, not clearing: Hansen's loss layer records canopy loss from any cause (Curtis et al., 2018). Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  5. The data go out under an open licence, and that is where a problem lies that the paper records as a gap. Statistics about named Indigenous lands can be quoted in contexts the communities did not choose: fire being more reactive inside protected areas than around them, for instance, can be read as the effect of conserved native vegetation or as mismanagement. The CARE principles for Indigenous data governance (Carroll et al., 2020) ask more than an open licence, and the suite does not yet consult Indigenous organisations on how these results are presented. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
